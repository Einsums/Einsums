Tutorial: From NumPy to an Optimized CCSD#

This tutorial takes one program, closed-shell CCSD with density-fitted integrals, from NumPy to a captured graph that the optimizer streams through a memory budget, keeps in files, and runs as a triangle. Each stage is a complete program in the ComputeGraph examples directory. The test suite runs all of them, and each one reproduces the first stage’s correlation energy to 1e-10. The code on this page is included from those files, so what you read is what runs.

The problem#

The molecule is water in cc-pVTZ: 5 occupied orbitals, 53 virtual, and 141 auxiliary functions for the density fitting. It comes from the fixture the DLPNO example carries, so nothing here needs an integral engine or psi4. ccsd_problem.py diagonalizes the converged Fock matrix for canonical orbitals and transforms the fitted three-index tensor to them, once, in NumPy. Every two-electron integral is then a product of two of its blocks,

\[\langle pq | rs \rangle = (pr|qs) = \sum_Q B^Q_{pr} B^Q_{qs}.\]

Water is small enough that every stage runs in a few seconds, which is what lets the test suite run them all, and large enough that the arithmetic shows in the timings. The \(v^4\) block of integrals is 63 MB here, which is what stages 5 and 6 are about: they are written for molecules where it is hundreds of gigabytes, and shown at a size where you can check every number.

Stage 1: the reference in NumPy#

The equations are the spin-adapted closed-shell ones of the psi4numpy RHF-CCSD, Crawford’s ccenergy formulation. The integral blocks are formed once:

# Without optimize, np.einsum runs a contraction as one loop nest; with it, NumPy hands each pair
# of operands to BLAS, which is what a reference worth timing against does.
einsum = functools.partial(np.einsum, optimize=True)

p = load()
no, nv = p.nocc, p.nvir
o, v = slice(0, no), slice(no, no + nv)
B = {"o": p.B[:, o, :], "v": p.B[:, v, :]}


def g(blocks):
    """The block <pq|rs> = (pr|qs) = sum_Q B[Q,p,r] B[Q,q,s], named like "oovv"."""
    P, Q, R, S = blocks
    left = B[P][:, :, v if R == "v" else o]
    right = B[Q][:, :, v if S == "v" else o]
    return einsum("Xpr,Xqs->pqrs", left, right)


oovv, ooov, oovo, oooo = g("oovv"), g("ooov"), g("oovo"), g("oooo")
ovvo, ovov, ovvv, vvvo, ovoo = g("ovvo"), g("ovov"), g("ovvv"), g("vvvo"), g("ovoo")
Bvv = B["v"][:, :, v]  # the v^4 block <ab|ef> is never formed; the ladder reads its factors

The iteration is a transcription of the equations. The one-particle intermediate \(F_{ae}\) reads like this:

Fae = np.diag(ev) + 2.0 * einsum("mf,mafe->ae", t1, ovvv) - einsum("mf,maef->ae", t1, ovvv)
Fae -= 2.0 * einsum("mnaf,mnef->ae", taut, oovv) - einsum("mnaf,mnfe->ae", taut, oovv)

and the doubles residual like this:

# The particle ladder, from the factors of <ab|ef> at every iteration. Written as one einsum
# over the three operands, NumPy's path search would refuse both ways of splitting it, since it
# caps an intermediate at the size of the largest input or output, and run one seven-index loop.
abef = einsum("Qae,Qbf->abef", Bvv, Bvv)
r2 = oovv + einsum("mnab,mnij->ijab", tau, Wmnij) + einsum("ijef,abef->ijab", tau, abef)
r2 += sym(einsum("ijae,be->ijab", t2, Fae - 0.5 * einsum("mb,me->be", t1, Fme)))
r2 -= sym(einsum("imab,mj->ijab", t2, Fmi + 0.5 * einsum("je,me->mj", t1, Fme)))
r2 -= sym(einsum("ma,mbij->ijab", t1, Zmbij))
r2 += sym(einsum("imae,mbej->ijab", t2 - t2.transpose(0, 1, 3, 2), Wmbej))
r2 += sym(einsum("imae,mbej->ijab", t2, Wmbej) + einsum("imae,mbje->ijab", t2, Wmbje))
r2 += sym(einsum("mjae,mbie->ijab", t2, Wmbje))
r2 -= sym(einsum("ie,ma,mbej->ijab", t1, t1, ovvo))
r2 -= sym(einsum("ie,mb,maje->ijab", t1, t1, ovov))
r2 += sym(einsum("ie,abej->ijab", t1, vvvo)) - sym(einsum("ma,mbij->ijab", t1, ovoo))

The particle ladder is the one term that would read the \(v^4\) block of integrals, and it is the term density fitting exists for. Here the block is formed from its factors at every iteration rather than stored, which is how a DF-CCSD code runs once \(v^4\) does not fit. NumPy will not split the three-operand contraction by itself: its path search caps an intermediate at the size of the largest input or output, refuses both ways of splitting this one, and falls back to a single seven-index loop. Spelling the two steps out is the NumPy programmer’s job.

The program checks itself before anything else depends on it. The MP2 energy of the first amplitudes agrees with psi4’s DF-MP2 for the same molecule to every printed digit, and the converged CCSD energy agrees with psi4’s DF-CCSD to 1.3e-7. That last difference is understood: the fixture’s SCF was density fitted in a different auxiliary basis than its correlation treatment, and with both fitted in cc-pVTZ-RI the same equations, on psi4’s own orbitals and integrals, agree with psi4 to 1e-11.

MP2  correlation energy = -0.2752048956   psi4 DF-MP2 = -0.2752048956
CCSD correlation energy = -0.2811389391   psi4 DF-CCSD = -0.2811388115   (27 iterations)

Stage 2: the same equations with Einsums#

ccsd_equations.py is stage 1 transcribed into Einsums calls. Every call in it runs immediately outside a capture and records a node inside one, so it is written once and used by every stage that follows.

Three things change in the transcription, and each is what a NumPy program has to give up to be captured.

An operation writes into a tensor you supply. einsums.einsum takes its output, with a prefactor on the output and one on the product. Two helpers name the only two cases the equations need:

def assign(out, spec, A, B, pf=1.0):
    """out = pf * A B."""
    einsums.einsum(spec, out, A, B, c_pf=0.0, ab_pf=pf)


def add(out, spec, A, B, pf=1.0):
    """out += pf * A B."""
    einsums.einsum(spec, out, A, B, c_pf=1.0, ab_pf=pf)

The first term of an intermediate assigns it and the rest add to it. That is a habit worth keeping even eagerly: a graph replays the same calls on the same tensors, so a sum that began from a tensor zeroed once, when it was made, would start the second replay from the first replay’s value.

Every tensor comes from a function the caller supplies. iterate() asks new(name, shape) for each intermediate rather than allocating one itself, which is what lets the later stages decide what an intermediate is without touching the equations. Here is \(F_{ae}\) again:

Fae = new("Fae", (nv, nv))
la.axpby(1.0, g["Fvv"], 0.0, Fae)
add(Fae, "a,e <- m,f ; m,a,f,e", t1, g["ovvv"], 2.0)
add(Fae, "a,e <- m,f ; m,a,e,f", t1, g["ovvv"], -1.0)
add(Fae, "a,e <- m,n,a,f ; m,n,e,f", taut, g["oovv"], -2.0)
add(Fae, "a,e <- m,n,a,f ; m,n,f,e", taut, g["oovv"], 1.0)

The amplitudes are updated in place. Rebinding a name, as stage 1 does with t1, t2 = iterate(t1, t2), changes nothing that a replay reads. The update divides the residuals by the denominators and adds the step into t1 and t2 themselves:

# The diagonal Fock terms in Fae and Fmi put -t D in the residual, so t + r / D is the update.
d1, d2 = new("d1", OV), new("d2", OOVV)
la.direct_division(1.0, r1, g["Dia"], 0.0, d1)
la.direct_division(1.0, r2, g["Dijab"], 0.0, d2)
la.axpby(1.0, d1, 1.0, t1)
la.axpby(1.0, d2, 1.0, t2)

Two things got simpler. The closed-shell residual symmetrizes most of its terms under the exchange \((i,a) \leftrightarrow (j,b)\), and symmetrizing is linear, so the terms are collected in one tensor X and symmetrized once, with one permute. And the ladder is written the way the equation reads, as one contraction over three operands:

r2 = new("r2", OOVV)
la.axpby(1.0, g["oovv"], 0.0, r2)
add(r2, "i,j,a,b <- m,n,a,b ; m,n,i,j", tau, Wmnij)
# The particle ladder, sum_ef tau[i,j,e,f] <ab|ef>, with <ab|ef> = sum_Q B[Q,a,e] B[Q,b,f].
einsums.einsum("i,j,a,b <- i,j,e,f ; Q,a,e ; Q,b,f", r2, tau, g["Bvv"], g["Bvv"], c_pf=1.0)
la.axpby(1.0, X, 1.0, r2)
einsums.permute("j,i,b,a <- i,j,a,b", r2, X, c_pf=1.0)

Einsums plans an einsum over three or more operands itself, choosing the order of the pairwise contractions, and limits a partial product by the memory budget, --einsums:max-memory, rather than by the size of the inputs. Eagerly the plan is chosen by the extents in front of it; captured, by the index spaces the operands are annotated with, which is stage 4’s subject.

The driver is the loop, with new making ordinary tensors:

def new(name, shape):
    return einsums.zeros(list(shape), name=name)


t1 = new("t1", (p.nocc, p.nvir))
t2 = new("t2", (p.nocc, p.nocc, p.nvir, p.nvir))
einsums.linalg.direct_division(1.0, ints["oovv"], ints["Dijab"], 0.0, t2)  # the MP2 guess
e = new("e", (1,))


def step():
    iterate(t1, t2, ints, new)
    energy(e, t1, t2, ints, new)


iterations, ms = converge(step, lambda: float(e[0]))

converge is a few lines in ccsd_equations.py that call the step until the energy settles and time it. The energy is the same, in the same 27 iterations. This stage is the reference semantics for everything after it: eager execution is what a captured graph has to reproduce.

Stage 3: capture once, replay#

A graph records the calls instead of running them. Wrap the same iterate() and energy() in a capture, optimize what was recorded, and replay it once per iteration:

def new(name, shape):
    return einsums.zeros(list(shape), name=name)


graph = cg.Graph("ccsd")
with cg.capture(graph):
    iterate(t1, t2, ints, new)
    energy(e, t1, t2, ints, new)
graph.optimize()

iterations, ms = converge(graph.execute, lambda: float(e[0]))

That is the whole change. t1, t2 and e are the caller’s tensors, made before the capture, so the graph reads and writes them where they are and the loop reads the energy from e after each replay. The loop itself stays in Python; graph.execute is just the step it calls.

optimize() is not optional here, and it is worth knowing why. The planner’s partial products for the ladder are tensors the graph declares and allocates later, and the default pipeline is what allocates them. A graph is meant to be optimized before it runs.

Inside a capture, einsums.zeros makes a tensor the graph owns as a result: the caller holds it and may read it after execute(), so the graph has to keep all of it. Every tensor iterate() asks new for in this stage is such a result. That is correct, and it ties the optimizer’s hands, which is the next stage’s subject.

Stage 4: tell the optimizer what the program is#

The optimizer can only rewrite what the program has told it it may. Stage 4 adds two declarations and nothing else.

Intermediates are scratch. graph.scratch declares a tensor that exists only during a replay:

register_spaces()
graph = cg.Graph("ccsd")


def new(name, shape):
    return graph.scratch(name, list(shape), "float64")


with cg.capture(graph):
    annotate(t1, t2, ints)
    iterate(t1, t2, ints, new)
    energy(e, t1, t2, ints, new)
graph.optimize()

A scratch tensor may be freed after its last use, share its memory with another, or be held a block at a time. In exchange its values mean nothing outside a replay: reading one from Python after execute(), or passing it to an eager operation, is refused with an error naming the tensor and the graph. e, the one value the loop reads, is the caller’s tensor and so is unaffected.

Axes have spaces. annotate says what each input’s axes range over, from three registered index spaces:

def register_spaces():
    """The three index spaces, with extents for the family of molecules the program is for."""
    registry = cg.global_space_registry()
    for name, symbol, extent, dim in (("occ", "o", 50.0, "nocc"), ("vir", "v", 450.0, "nvir"), ("aux", "Q", 1500.0, "naux")):
        if registry.find(name) is None:
            registry.register_space(cg.index_space(name, symbol, extent, cg.GrowthClass.linear(), dim))


def annotate(t1, t2, ints):
    """Say what every input's axes range over. Called inside the capture."""
    space = {"o": "occ", "v": "vir", "Q": "aux"}
    blocks = {"t1": "ov", "t2": "oovv", "Bvv": "Qvv", "Foo": "oo", "Fvv": "vv", "Dia": "ov", "Dijab": "oovv"}
    for tensor, letters in ((t1, blocks["t1"]), (t2, blocks["t2"])):
        cg.annotate(tensor, tuple(space[x] for x in letters))
    for name, tensor in ints.items():
        cg.annotate(tensor, tuple(space[x] for x in blocks.get(name, name)))

A space’s extent is not this molecule’s size. It describes the family of molecules the program is for, here a few hundred basis functions, so that a pass weighing two forms of a contraction weighs them where the program will run rather than at water. Only the inputs are annotated; the passes carry the spaces through every intermediate.

graph.explain reports what the optimizer did:

Read it from the top.

  • The spin adaptation writes many terms as 2 A - B, where A and B are the same integral with two indices exchanged. LinearCombinationContractionFolding found nine such pairs and runs each as one contraction against the combined integral.

  • The cost of an iteration is a polynomial in the spaces’ symbols, and the rate-limiting node is the ladder at \(2 o^2 v^4\). The term \(2 v^4 Q\) is the planner’s choice for the ladder: with the spaces annotated it forms \(\langle ab|ef\rangle\) from its factors and then contracts it, the order with the fewest operations for this family.

  • The footprint has a \(v^4\) term, the price of that choice: 63 MB of the 71 MB peak. It is the subject of stage 5.

  • The memory passes free every intermediate after its last use and pack 21 of them into one arena, which is held whole for the replay, so the peak counts all of it.

The same report is available as records a program can read, graph.decisions, each with a stable code; Tutorial: The Algebraic Optimizer describes them.

Stage 5: run in less memory than the program names#

The ladder’s \(v^4\) intermediate is 63 MB at water and hundreds of gigabytes for a molecule CCSD is run on. Tell the program how much memory it has:

# The runtime reads the budget when it starts, so it is set before einsums is imported.
einsums.rc.max_memory = "64MB"

The runtime reads the budget when it starts, so it is set before einsums is imported; --einsums:max-memory on the command line or EINSUMS_MAX_MEMORY in the environment do the same. The capture is stage 4’s, unchanged, and the report says what changed:

slicing Bvv*Bvv[1] into 53 slice(s), blocks of 18 in 2 loop(s) of 3 iteration(s) in all, seed 63123848 bytes -> largest intermediate 21438288 bytes

AxisTiling turned the ladder into a loop over blocks of one of its virtual indices, so \(\langle ab|ef\rangle\) is built and used 21.4 MB at a time instead of 63 MB. The block is the one the optimizer predicts runs fastest within the budget, pricing each candidate’s contractions at the shape its tiles have. Nothing in the equations moved, and the energy is the same to every digit. A program that fits its budget is left alone, which is why stage 4, with the machine’s whole memory as its budget, was not tiled.

A budget has to leave room for what cannot be sliced. The program’s inputs, the integral blocks, amplitudes and denominators, hold 18.1 MB from the first statement to the last; an intermediate counts only from its first writer to its last reader. Under a 32 MB budget the ladder’s loop takes smaller blocks of the same index, 8.3 MB of \(\langle ab|ef\rangle\) at a time, and runs within 5% of the 64 MB loop. Under 16 MB the inputs alone are over the budget, and no loop can help; the replay says so:

graph 'ccsd': a replay held 25179784 bytes at its peak, 7101152 of graph-managed tensors and 18078632 resident throughout, over --einsums:max-memory of 16777216 bytes; the optimizer could not fit the program to the budget

A budget smaller than the inputs is the next stage’s problem.

Stage 6: integrals and amplitudes in files#

Tiling bounds what the program builds. The inputs are a different problem: at a large enough molecule the integral blocks themselves do not fit, and amplitudes that took hours to converge are worth keeping where a crash cannot take them. A backing says where a tensor lives instead of memory:

ints_path, amps_path = os.path.join(scratch, "ints.etn"), os.path.join(scratch, "amps.etn")
for name in on_disk:
    einsums.io.write(ints_path, name, eager[name])
t2_guess = einsums.zeros([p.nocc, p.nocc, p.nvir, p.nvir], name="t2")
einsums.linalg.direct_division(1.0, eager["oovv"], eager["Dijab"], 0.0, t2_guess)
einsums.io.write(amps_path, "t1", einsums.zeros([p.nocc, p.nvir], name="t1"))
einsums.io.write(amps_path, "t2", t2_guess)

graph = cg.Graph("ccsd")
files, amplitudes = einsums.io.EtnFile(ints_path), einsums.io.EtnFile(amps_path)
ints = {name: graph.declare_tensor(name, backing=files.entry(name)) for name in on_disk}
ints.update({name: eager[name] for name in ("Foo", "Fvv", "Dia", "Dijab")})
t1 = graph.declare_tensor("t1", backing=amplitudes.entry("t1", update=True))
t2 = graph.declare_tensor("t2", backing=amplitudes.entry("t2", update=True))

The integral blocks are written once and declared on the graph with read backings: each replay reads them from the file, and the caller never holds them. t1 and t2 have update backings: each replay starts from what the file holds and leaves the file holding the new amplitudes. The new values are written beside the old ones and published in one commit at the end of the replay, so a crash leaves the previous iteration’s amplitudes, and a later run can start from them. iterate() and energy() are handed these tensors and do not know the difference.

The budget is 16 MB, less than the program’s inputs, which is now possible because the inputs no longer have to be in memory. The report says how each one is moved:

The ladder is sliced again, in smaller blocks; the 6 MB ovvv block is read a slab at a time by every contraction that uses it; and inside the ladder’s loop, Bvv is read from the file one box at a time, the box each block needs. graph.last_run() says what a replay actually moved:

run = graph.last_run()
print(f"the last replay held at most {run.peak_bytes} bytes")
for backing in run.backings:
    print(f"  {backing.tensor}: {backing.bytes_read} bytes read in {backing.reads} read(s), {backing.bytes_written} written")

  ovvv: 35730480 bytes read in 18 read(s), 0 written

The replay held 15.9 MB at most, against 66 MB in stage 4. That was paid for in reading: Bvv is 3.2 MB and was read 9 times over, because every block of the ladder reads the box it needs. The budget steers what is streamed, and is not a hard limit on what a replay holds. And the commit is not free either: each replay waits for the drive three times, once for each amplitude and once for the commit that publishes both.

Stage 7: declare the symmetry the amplitudes have#

The closed-shell amplitude is unchanged when the two electrons trade places, \(t_{ij}^{ab} = t_{ji}^{ba}\). Stage 7 is stage 4 with that said:

t2.set_symmetry(einsums.SymmetryDescriptor([einsums.SymmetryOp.group_swap([0, 2], [1, 3])]))

From the declaration the optimizer proves that the ladder’s contribution has the same symmetry, and computes only the blocks of it on and above the diagonal, writing each one’s mirror from it:

runs 36 of 64 tile(s) of 7 x 7, writing each off-diagonal tile's mirror with sign +1
slicing Bvv*Bvv[0] x Bvv*Bvv[2] into 2809 slice(s), a triangle of blocks of 7 x 7 in 4 loop(s) of 36 iteration(s) in all, seed 63123848 bytes -> largest intermediate 1101128 bytes

A declaration is a promise, and the tiled loop checks it at every replay before relying on it. The energy is unchanged.

Stage 8: files and symmetry together#

The declarations compose. Stage 8 is stage 6 with stage 7’s line, made on the t2 the graph declared with its file backing:

t2.set_symmetry(einsums.SymmetryDescriptor([einsums.SymmetryOp.group_swap([0, 2], [1, 3])]))

A symmetry declared on a tensor the graph declared is read when the graph is optimized, so it may be set after the declaration, as here, or after the capture. The ladder runs as a triangle again, now with its factor Bvv read from the file a box at a time inside the loop:

runs 36 of 64 tile(s) of 7 x 7, writing each off-diagonal tile's mirror with sign +1
slicing Bvv*Bvv[0] x Bvv*Bvv[2] into 2809 slice(s), a triangle of blocks of 7 x 7 in 4 loop(s) of 36 iteration(s) in all, seed 63123848 bytes -> largest intermediate 1101128 bytes

and what the replay read from the file falls with it:

the last replay held at most 8440616 bytes
  Bvv: 28516968 bytes read in 72 read(s), 0 written
  ...

Bvv is read in 72 pieces here against stage 6’s 24, and the same 29 MB: the triangle skips the blocks below the diagonal, but its blocks are smaller, so each piece is too. The blocks are stage 7’s: the 16 MB budget has room for them and for the mirror’s scratch tile beside everything else.

Read it as six observations rather than a ranking.

Eager Einsums is not faster than good NumPy. The two run the same algorithm on the same BLAS, and come out even. Stage 2 exists to fix the semantics, not the speed.

The speed comes from capturing, and from what you declare. Replaying the captured iteration is 1.6 times faster than NumPy, and declaring scratch and spaces takes that to 1.9. None of it touched the equations.

Holding less need not cost time. Stage 5 holds a 21.4 MB block of \(\langle ab|ef\rangle\) where stage 4 holds all 63 MB, and runs 13% faster.

Files are bought with time. Stage 6 holds a quarter of what stage 4 holds and takes about three times as long. The difference is the ladder’s loop, which reads each block’s box of Bvv from the file, 9 times its size in all, the slabs of ovvv and the other integral blocks, and the three waits for the drive that make each commit safe. At a molecule whose integrals do not fit in memory, there is no faster version to compare it with.

The triangle pays at water too. It halves the ladder’s arithmetic, and stage 7 is 18% faster than stage 4. How large the blocks should be is the optimizer’s choice, and it is not the arithmetic that decides it. A block of the ladder’s output is computed by a contraction of its own, and a small block pays for more contractions, more packing of their operands, and more of the mirror’s copies; a large block computes more of the diagonal twice. The optimizer prices each candidate block as the contraction engine would run its tiles, from the hardware profile when einsums tune has measured one and from a built-in table otherwise, and here it picks blocks of 7 x 7. On benzene in cc-pVDZ, where the ladder is most of an iteration, the same declaration took an iteration to 0.69 to 0.72 of the time without it.

With files, the triangle pays less. Stage 8 is 4% faster than stage 6. The triangle halves the ladder’s arithmetic, but with files the ladder is a smaller part of the iteration: Bvv is read in the same 29 MB either way, and the reads of the other integrals and the waits for the commit stay as they were.
